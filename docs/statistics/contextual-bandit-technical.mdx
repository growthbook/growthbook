Here we document the technical details behind GrowthBook's contextual bandit variation weight updates. For a product-level introduction, see [Contextual Bandits](/bandits/contextual); to run one, see [Setting up a Contextual Bandit](/bandits/contextual-config).

GrowthBook fits a contextual bandit in two stages. First it partitions users into **leaves** by greedily growing a decision tree over the context attributes. Then it runs **Thompson sampling** _within each leaf_ to set that leaf's variation weights. The sections below describe the two stages in that order.

## Growing the Context Tree (BIC / EBIC)

GrowthBook defines the leaves by greedily growing a binary decision tree over the context attributes, using an Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) to decide when to stop splitting so the tree does not overfit the context.

### Model and notation

Within each leaf, a variation's metric values are modeled as independent Gaussian draws around a leaf-and-variation-specific mean. Index the variations with sufficient data by $v = 1, \dots, K$. Let $n_v$ be variation $v$'s total sample size across the tree and $N = \sum_{v} n_v$.

For a given tree, let $\mathrm{SSE}_v$ be variation $v$'s residual sum of squares, pooled across all current leaves (each leaf's residuals taken around that leaf's sample mean for the arm). We leverage EBIC to minimize $\mathrm{SSE}_v$ while controlling for the number of model parameters. Our approach does not require the raw data to be Gaussian, only the sample means, as we describe in [Appendix: Deriving the Profile Log-Likelihood](#appendix-deriving-the-profile-log-likelihood).

The profiled Gaussian log-likelihood for arm $v$ (maximized over the variance) is

$$
\ell_v = -\frac{n_v}{2}\left[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{SSE}_v}{n_v}\right) + 1\right].
$$

### A single split

Each binary split of a leaf gives every arm its own mean in each of the two children, adding $K$ parameters (one mean per arm). Let $\mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{before}}$ be the arm's pooled SSE before the split and $\mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{after}}$ the pooled SSE afterward (the sum of the two children's SSE). Because the per-arm sample sizes are unchanged by a split, the deviance (likelihood-ratio) improvement reduces to

$$
G^2 = \sum_{v=1}^{K} n_v \left[\ln \mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{before}} - \ln \mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{after}}\right] = \sum_{v=1}^{K} n_v \ln\!\frac{\mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{before}}}{\mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{after}}},
$$

which equals twice the increase in the maximized log-likelihood.

### BIC stopping gate

BIC adds a complexity penalty $p \ln N$ for a model with $p$ parameters. A split adds $K$ parameters, so its BIC change is

$$
\Delta \mathrm{BIC} = K \ln N - G^2.
$$

GrowthBook accepts a split only when it lowers BIC — that is, when $\Delta\mathrm{BIC} < 0$, equivalently when the deviance improvement clears the penalty:

$$
G^2 > K \ln N.
$$

### EBIC multiplicity correction

At each growth step GrowthBook does not test a single pre-specified split. It searches every candidate split across all current leaves and their attributes and keeps the best one. Taking the arg-max over many candidates inflates the apparent improvement, so plain BIC under-penalizes. The Extended BIC ([Chen & Chen, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/asn034)) adds a term for the size of the searched model space. GrowthBook adds $2\gamma \ln M$ to the penalty:

$$
\mathrm{penalty} = K \ln N + 2\gamma \ln M, \qquad \Delta\mathrm{BIC} = \mathrm{penalty} - G^2,
$$

where

- $M$ is the number of candidate binary splits searched at that step, summed over all current leaves and their attributes (an attribute with $c \ge 2$ distinct values in a leaf contributes $c$ candidates), floored at $1$.
- $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is the EBIC parameter. $\gamma = 0$ recovers plain BIC; $\gamma = 1$ is a Bonferroni-style correction across the candidates searched. The default is $\gamma = 1$.

### Growth algorithm

Starting from the root (a single leaf), at each step GrowthBook:

1. finds, for every current leaf, the candidate split that most reduces total SSE, then takes the global best across leaves;
2. stops if no valid split remains or the best split does not strictly reduce SSE;
3. computes $\Delta\mathrm{BIC}$ for that split using the EBIC penalty above, stops if $\Delta\mathrm{BIC} \ge 0$, and otherwise accepts the split and continues.

Growth also stops once the tree reaches its maximum number of leaves. Because the penalty scales with $K \ln N$ (plus $2\gamma \ln M$), larger experiments must clear a proportionally larger deviance improvement to justify each additional split, which keeps the tree conservative as data accrue.

## Thompson Sampling

Once the tree is built, GrowthBook sets the variation weights _within each leaf_ by Thompson sampling, using the posteriors formed from that leaf's data.

Suppose we have $K$ variations, and we want to update the variation weights for a given context.  
For the $k$-th variation, define its mean as $\mu_k$, k = 1, ..., K.
Denote the probability that $\mu_{k}$ is larger than the other means as $w_k$.  
Under Thompson Sampling, $w_k$ is the variation weight for the $k$-th variation.
Define the posterior mean of $\mu_k$ as $m_k$ and its posterior variance as $v_k^2$.  
Define its posterior standard deviation as $s_k = \sqrt{v_k^2}$.
Define the posterior distribution of $\mu_k$ as $f_k(\mu_k)$.

This best-arm probability can be expressed as:

$$
\begin{align*}
w_k &= P\left(\left[\mu_k > \mu_1 \right] \cap \left[\mu_k > \mu_2 \right] \cap \cdots \cap \left[\mu_k > \mu_{k-1} \right] \cap \left[\mu_k > \mu_{k+1} \right] \cap \cdots \cap \left[\mu_k > \mu_K \right]\right)
\\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{k}}\int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{k}}    \cdots  \int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{k}}  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}   \int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{1}}  \cdots  \int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{1}} \prod_{i=1}^{K} f_{i}(\mu_i) d\mu_i
\\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{k}(\mu_k) \left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{k}} \cdots \int_{-\infty}^{\mu_{k}} \prod_{i\ne k} f_{i}(\mu_i) d\mu_i  \right\} d\mu_k
\\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{k}(\mu_k) \prod_{i\ne k} \Phi\left(\frac{\mu_{k} - m_{i} }{s_{i}}\right)   d\mu_k.
\end{align*}
$$

We leverage the fact that the posterior distributions of the $\mu_i$ are normal and independent.

The result above can be expressed as the expected value of the function below over the distribution of $\mu_k$

$$
\prod_{i\ne k} \Phi\left(\frac{\mu_{k} - m_{i} }{s_{i}}\right).
$$

We use Gauss-Hermite quadrature to approximate the integral.
Our integral is well suited for Gauss-Hermite quadrature, as the function inside the integral is smooth and light-tailed.  
Let $$t = \frac{\mu_{k} - m_{k} }{s_{k}},$$
which implies that
$$\mu_{k} = m_{k} + t s_{k}.$$

Define the standard normal distribution as $\phi(.)$.
The integral can be represented as:

$$
\begin{align*}
w_k &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left\{ \prod_{i\ne k} \Phi\left(\frac{m_{k} + t s_{k} - m_{i} }{s_{i}}\right)\right\}   \phi(t)  dt.
\end{align*}
$$

Letting $u = t / \sqrt(2)$ places the integral in the standard form for the Gauss-Hermite quadrature, which is

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp^{-u^2} g(u) du.
$$

### Prior Sample Size and Exploration

By default each arm's posterior is formed from its own data under a diffuse conjugate prior, and the arms' posteriors are independent — which is exactly what the best-arm probability above assumes. The optional **prior sample size** parameter $n_0$ (seen in the "Increase Bandit Exploration" setting) replaces that with a hierarchical model that partially pools the arms toward a shared mean, trading some exploitation for extra exploration.

Within a leaf, let arm $k$ have sample size $n_k$, sample mean $\bar y_k$, and distributional variance $\sigma_k^2$. The hierarchical model is

$$
\bar\mu \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2), \qquad
\mu_k \mid \bar\mu \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\bar\mu,\, \frac{\sigma_k^2}{n_0}\right), \qquad
\bar y_k \mid \mu_k \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\mu_k,\, \frac{\sigma_k^2}{n_k}\right),
$$

where $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0^2$ are the diffuse hyperprior mean and variance. The middle line says the common-mean prior for each arm is worth $n_0$ pseudo-observations, which is the sense in which $n_0$ is a "prior sample size."

Writing the data precision as $\rho_k = n_k / \sigma_k^2$ and the shrinkage factor as

$$
B_k = \frac{n_0}{n_k + n_0},
$$

the shared mean has posterior

$$
\hat{\bar\mu} = \frac{\sigma_0^{-2}\mu_0 + \sum_k \rho_k B_k\, \bar y_k}{\sigma_0^{-2} + \sum_k \rho_k B_k}, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\bar\mu}) = \left(\sigma_0^{-2} + \sum_k \rho_k B_k\right)^{-1},
$$

and each arm's posterior mean and variance (the $m_k$ and $s_k^2$ fed into the Thompson weights) are

$$
m_k = (1 - B_k)\,\bar y_k + B_k\,\hat{\bar\mu}, \qquad
s_k^2 = \frac{\sigma_k^2}{n_k + n_0} + B_k^2 \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\bar\mu}).
$$

Larger $n_0$ increases $B_k$, pulling every arm's posterior mean toward the common mean and flattening the resulting weights, which increases exploration. Setting $n_0 = 0$ gives $B_k = 0$, so $m_k = \bar y_k$ and the arms decouple, recovering the independent-arm setup above.

**Approximation when $n_0 > 0$.** The best-arm probability formula treats the posterior arm means as independent (it integrates a product of marginal normal CDFs). This is exact only when $n_0 = 0$. When $n_0 > 0$, every arm is shrunk toward the same estimated common mean $\hat{\bar\mu}$, which makes the posterior arm means **positively dependent** through that shared term. GrowthBook still computes the weights by plugging the marginal posterior means $m_k$ and standard deviations $s_k$ into the independent-arm quadrature, so **the Thompson probabilities are approximate** — they do not account for the dependence among the means. Because the shared-mean uncertainty is common to all arms, ignoring it overstates the variance of the pairwise differences $\mu_k - \mu_j$, which nudges the probabilities slightly toward uniform (a small additional amount of exploration beyond the shrinkage itself).

## Appendix: Deriving the Profile Log-Likelihood

The [tree-growing criterion](#growing-the-context-tree-bic-/-ebic) scores each arm with the profiled Gaussian log-likelihood

$$
\ell_v = -\frac{n_v}{2}\left[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{SSE}_v}{n_v}\right) + 1\right].
$$

Here is where that comes from. Fix an arm $v$. Under the Gaussian model each observation $y_{v,j}$ ($j = 1, \dots, n_v$) is drawn around the mean of whichever leaf $\ell(j)$ it falls in, with a common within-arm variance $\sigma_v^2$, so that $y_{v,j} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{v,\ell(j)}, \sigma_v^2)$. The log-likelihood over the leaf means $\{\mu_{v,\ell}\}$ and the variance $\sigma_v^2$ is

$$
\ell_v(\{\mu_{v,\ell}\}, \sigma_v^2) = -\frac{n_v}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{n_v}{2}\ln\sigma_v^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma_v^2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_v}\bigl(y_{v,j} - \mu_{v,\ell(j)}\bigr)^2.
$$

**Step 1 — profile out the means.** Only the final term depends on the means, and it is minimized leaf-by-leaf by the within-leaf sample mean, $\hat\mu_{v,\ell} = \bar y_{v,\ell}$. Substituting these, the residual sum of squares is

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{n_v}\bigl(y_{v,j} - \hat\mu_{v,\ell(j)}\bigr)^2 = \mathrm{SSE}_v.
$$

**Step 2 — profile out the variance.** Differentiating the concentrated log-likelihood with respect to $\sigma_v^2$ and setting it to zero,

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_v}{\partial \sigma_v^2} = -\frac{n_v}{2\sigma_v^2} + \frac{\mathrm{SSE}_v}{2\sigma_v^4} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\sigma_v^2 = \frac{\mathrm{SSE}_v}{n_v}.
$$

**Step 3 — substitute back.** The quadratic term collapses to a constant, $\dfrac{\mathrm{SSE}_v}{2\hat\sigma_v^2} = \dfrac{n_v}{2}$, which yields the profiled form above.

The split statistic follows immediately: comparing a tree to the same tree with one additional split, $n_v$ and the $\ln(2\pi) + 1$ constants are identical before and after, so

$$
G^2 = 2\sum_v\bigl(\ell_v^{\text{after}} - \ell_v^{\text{before}}\bigr) = \sum_v n_v\left[\ln\mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{before}} - \ln\mathrm{SSE}_v^{\text{after}}\right].
$$

### Why only the means need to be normal

The derivation above assumes the individual observations are i.i.d. normal, which is what makes $\ell_v$ the exact MLE. But the procedure needs much less than that. The criterion only ever touches the data through **group means and sums of squares**, so maximizing $\ell_v$ is identical to minimizing $\mathrm{SSE}_v$ — a least-squares (Gaussian quasi-likelihood) score that is sensible for any outcome with finite variance. What actually justifies the downstream inference — $G^2$ behaving like a likelihood ratio, and BIC's $\ln N$ penalty being the right scale — is the asymptotic normality of the **estimated means**, not of the raw outcomes.

So the operative requirement is simply that the **sample means are approximately Gaussian**, which the Central Limit Theorem delivers whenever each leaf has enough units and the metric has finite variance. This is the same assumption the Thompson-sampling stage makes (normal posteriors on the $\mu_k$), so both stages rest on one consistent approximation. Consequently:

- **The raw data need not be Gaussian.** Skewed, bounded, or binary metrics are fine as long as leaves are large enough for their means to be near-normal.
- **This is why the per-leaf minimum-units requirement matters** — it keeps each leaf mean in the regime where the normal approximation (and hence both the BIC score and the Thompson weights) is trustworthy.
- **The pooled variance assumes homoscedasticity.** Profiling to a single $\hat\sigma_v^2 = \mathrm{SSE}_v / n_v$ per arm treats the within-arm variance as roughly constant across leaves; if context-level variances differ substantially, plain SSE is a (usually mild) approximation of a variance-weighted criterion.
