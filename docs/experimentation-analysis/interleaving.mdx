## Why interleaving

Search, recommendation, and feed teams regularly iterate on their **rankers** with new models, new data, and new parameters and weights. A mature ranker is already good, so most changes have small effects, and small effects need a lot of users. Testing rankers one A/B test at a time becomes the bottleneck. Offline evaluation is fast, but it is a [poor predictor](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3292500.3330744) of which ranker wins with real users.

Interleaving sits between the offline evaluation and proper A/B testing. Instead of showing ranker A to one group of users and ranker B to another, it **merges both rankers' lists into one** and shows that list to everyone. Every user compares both rankers, so each user is their own control. Companies that run interleaving report needing a small fraction of the traffic a standard experiment needs:

| Company                                                                                            | Traffic needed vs. an A/B test |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| [Netflix](https://netflixtechblog.com/interleaving-in-online-experiments-at-netflix-a04ee392ec55)  | under 1%                       |
| [DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/doordash-experimentation-with-interleaving-designs/) | about 1.5%                     |
| [Amazon](https://www.amazon.science/publications/debiased-balanced-interleaving-at-amazon-search)  | about 1.7%                     |
| [Airbnb](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00751)                                                         | about 2%                       |

In marketplaces, interleaving also avoids a bias that A/B tests on rankers suffer from: users who see ranker B can book supply that users on ranker A would otherwise have booked. With interleaving both rankers face the same supply on every search. Other solutions to marketplace interference, such as synthetic controls or cluster randomization, require hard-to-justify assumptions and/or data-hungry approaches.

## How it fits into your experimentation program

Interleaving answers a narrower question than an A/B test: **which ranker's items do users pick more often?** It does not tell you how much a business metric would change if you shipped the winner, because no user ever sees either ranker on its own.

Most teams use it as a screen:

1. **Offline evaluation** to discard the clearly bad candidates.
2. **Interleaving** to compare the remaining candidates with real users, on a small share of traffic.
3. **An A/B test** (or a switchback or synethic control test where interference is a risk) on the one or two finalists to measure the effect size before shipping or as periodic validation.

Once you have a track record of interleaving and A/B tests agreeing on your surface, you can rely on interleaving alone more often. See [Understanding interleaving results](/experimentation-analysis/interleaving/results#how-interleaving-relates-to-ab-test-results) for when and why the two can disagree.

## How it works

### Team draft

GrowthBook merges the two lists with **team-draft interleaving**, the method Netflix, Airbnb, and DoorDash use. It works like two team captains picking players. In every round, a coin flip decides which ranker picks first, and each ranker takes the highest item on its list that is not in the merged list yet. The user sees one list and has no way to tell that two rankers are at play.

There is one special case. If both rankers want the same item in the same round, it goes in once and belongs to neither ranker. An item both rankers agree on cannot tell you which ranker is better, so engagement with it counts for neither. GrowthBook calls items that belong to a ranker **competitive** and the rest **non-competitive**. Items that fill out the list after one ranker runs out are non-competitive too.

Team drafting has two attractive properties: (1) every competitive round gives each ranker exactly one item, so both get the same number of competitive items; (2) a coin flip decides who picks first, so neither ranker gets the higher position of a pair more often than the other.

### Two ways to pick a winner

The draft decides what the user sees and whether an item in the list is competitive (e.g. helps us differentiate between rankers). There are two different ways, then, to attribute positive outcomes with one ranker vs the other:

- **Paired analysis** compares the two rankers within each merged list and requires the search/query ID (what we internally call the `ranking_id`) to be tracked with the exposure and with all downstream engagements within the list. An engagement with a competitive item credits the ranker that drafted the item, and the test asks whether one ranker earns more engagements on competitive items than the other. It is the most sensitive option and fits in-context signals such as clicks, add-to-cart, or orders placed straight from the list.
- **Preference analysis** gives each user one vote for the ranker whose items they engaged with more across the whole experiment. It fits outcomes that build up over several visits, such as a booking after a week of searching, and it keeps a few very active users from deciding the result. There are a few different ways to determine how user-level preference is counted up across multiple queries/rankings, which we cover in [Understanding interleaving results](/experimentation-analysis/interleaving/results).

You can run both in the same experiment, for example paired analysis on clicks and preference analysis on orders. [Understanding interleaving results](/experimentation-analysis/interleaving/results) covers both in detail.
